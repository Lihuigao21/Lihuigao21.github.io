Quantum Dynamics

Ordered and Kubo Correlations

2026.10.13 (updated 2026.10.15) - Thermal Weights / Imaginary Time / Harmonic Oscillator

A quantum correlation can change when its operators are reordered, or when the thermal density operator is split around an insertion. Yet the exact ordered and Kubo spectra can be converted into each other. This seems contradictory only if we confuse three questions: which correlation is defined, what information an exact correlation contains, and which observable an approximate calculation reproduces. A harmonic oscillator makes these distinctions explicit, including the surprisingly different behavior of the midpoint and symmetrized correlations at low temperature.

This note develops the physical meaning of those choices, then connects them to weak-coupling electronic transitions and RPMD. It complements the existing DVR Kubo calculation, RPMD harmonic benchmark, and Matsubara imaginary-time construction. The new question here is how the different exact correlations relate, and what that relationship permits us to infer.

1. What does a classical correlation become upon quantization?

A classical thermal correlation averages a product over initial phase-space points. For observables \(A\) and \(B\), classical Hamiltonian evolution supplies the later value \(B(t)\), and ordinary numbers satisfy \(AB(t)=B(t)A\). Quantizing the observables and evolution introduces operators, but does not by itself specify how to order their product. Quantizing a whole phase-space product under a particular prescription is also generally different from multiplying two separately quantized operators.

We therefore fix the convention before doing any calculation. Let \(H\) be the Hamiltonian, \(\beta=1/(k_BT)\), \(Z=\operatorname{Tr}e^{-\beta H}\), and

\[ \rho=\frac{e^{-\beta H}}{Z},\qquad B(t)=e^{iHt/\hbar}B e^{-iHt/\hbar},\qquad C_{AB}^{\mathrm{ord}}(t)=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho A B(t)]. \]

Here ordered means this fixed product order. It does not mean the time-ordering operation \(T\). The reverse-order correlation is \(\operatorname{Tr}[\rho B(t)A]\). Their difference is the thermal commutator. For Hermitian observables, the two are complex conjugates, and

\[ \operatorname{Re}C_{AB}^{\mathrm{ord}}(t) =\frac12\langle\{A,B(t)\}\rangle, \qquad \operatorname{Im}C_{AB}^{\mathrm{ord}}(t) =\frac{1}{2i}\langle[A,B(t)]\rangle. \]

The imaginary part need not signal an unphysical quantity: the product of two Hermitian operators need not be Hermitian. It carries the distinction between the two orders. These are valid, different quantum correlation definitions. A measurement or response calculation determines which combination is needed; validity does not make them interchangeable without a transformation.

2. Splitting the thermal state changes transition weights

Once order matters, we can ask precisely what happens if part of \(\rho\) is placed on the other side of \(A\). Consider the family

\[ C_{s,AB}(t)=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho^{1-s}A\rho^s B(t)], \qquad 0\leq s\leq1. \]

This is an exact definition, not an approximation to the thermal state. Both fractional powers refer to the same normalized Gibbs state. The endpoints give the two orders, since cyclicity of the trace gives \(C_{1,AB}(t)=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho B(t)A]\). Cyclicity allows a rotation of the complete product; it does not allow arbitrary exchanges of neighboring factors.

To see what the interior values mean, insert energy eigenstates \(H|n\rangle=E_n|n\rangle\). Write \(p_n=e^{-\beta E_n}/Z\), \(A_{nm}=\langle n|A|m\rangle\), and \(\omega_{mn}=(E_m-E_n)/\hbar\). Then

\[ C_{s,AB}(t)=\sum_{n,m}p_n^{1-s}p_m^s A_{nm}B_{mn}e^{i\omega_{mn}t}. \]

The same energy differences and matrix elements occur for every \(s\). What changes is their thermal weight. For an autocorrelation of a Hermitian \(A\), the matrix-element product is \(|A_{nm}|^2\), making the comparison especially transparent:

CorrelationWeight for the same pair of levels
Ordered, \(s=0\)\(p_n\)
Reverse ordered, \(s=1\)\(p_m\)
Midpoint, \(s=1/2\)\(\sqrt{p_np_m}\)
Anticommutator symmetrized\((p_n+p_m)/2\)
Kubo\(\int_0^1p_n^{1-s}p_m^s\,ds\)

The midpoint uses a geometric mean; the anticommutator uses an arithmetic mean. They are different even though both correlations are real for Hermitian observables. The Kubo correlation averages the entire splitting family:

\[ C_{AB}^{K}(t)=\int_0^1ds\,C_{s,AB}(t) =\frac{1}{\beta Z}\int_0^\beta d\lambda\, \operatorname{Tr}[e^{-(\beta-\lambda)H}A e^{-\lambda H}B(t)]. \]

Its transition weight is the logarithmic mean \((p_n-p_m)/(\ln p_n-\ln p_m)\), with the continuous value \(p_n\) for equal probabilities. Thus varying \(s\) does not change the temperature, Hamiltonian, or real-time evolution. It changes the bilinear correlation being evaluated. If \(A\) commutes with \(\rho\), all members of this family coincide; in general they do not.

3. Exact spectral conversion does not make the functions identical

Different transition weights raise a sharper question: is information destroyed by the Kubo average? For a Gibbs state, the ratio of two thermal probabilities depends only on their energy difference:

\[ \frac{p_m}{p_n}=e^{-\beta\hbar\omega_{mn}}. \]

Every contribution at a given frequency therefore acquires the same multiplier. Define the spectrum using the convention

\[ S_{s,AB}(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\, e^{-i\omega t}C_{s,AB}(t). \]

Fourier transforming the energy-basis expression, then integrating over \(s\), gives

\[ S_{s,AB}(\omega)=e^{-s\beta\hbar\omega}S_{0,AB}(\omega), \qquad S_{AB}^{K}(\omega)=f(\beta\hbar\omega)S_{0,AB}(\omega), \quad f(y)=\frac{1-e^{-y}}{y},\quad f(0)=1. \]

At known finite temperature, \(f(y)\) is nonzero at every finite real frequency. The complete exact ordered spectrum can therefore be recovered:

\[ \boxed{S_{0,AB}(\omega)= \frac{\beta\hbar\omega}{1-e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}} S_{AB}^{K}(\omega).} \]

This answers the information question at a specific level: the exact correlations carry equivalent two-point spectral information for the same pair of observables in thermal equilibrium. It does not say that their numerical values are equal, that either one determines every observable or higher-order correlation, or that one selected two-point function determines the complete quantum state and Hamiltonian. It also does not remove errors in approximate input spectra. These conventions and the Kubo conversion are consistent with Hele's review [1].

For a Hermitian autocorrelation, thermal detailed balance reads \(S_0(-\omega)=e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}S_0(\omega)\), while \(S_K(-\omega)=S_K(\omega)\). The missing-looking positive/negative-frequency asymmetry is restored by the known thermal factor. Its time-domain consequence is

\[ \operatorname{Im}C_0(t) =-\frac{\beta\hbar}{2}\frac{dC_K(t)}{dt}. \]

The Kubo function can be real and still determine this imaginary part. Its realness should not be confused with classical dynamics. For a perturbation \(H'=-h(t)A\), the linear response of \(A\) is

\[ R_{AA}(t)=\frac{i}{\hbar}\Theta(t)\langle[A(t),A]\rangle =-\beta\Theta(t)\frac{dC_K(t)}{dt}. \]

Here \(h(t)\) is the applied field and \(\Theta\) is the step function. Kubo correlations are connected to physical response through a specific operation. In practice, limited observation time, spectral truncation, and noise can make the inverse poorly resolved; differentiation and thermal reweighting can amplify errors. The strict zero-temperature limit is also singular: taking a vanishing raw Kubo function first need not preserve the information that was present at every finite temperature.

4. What does “halfway around the imaginary-time path” mean?

The fractional thermal factors can be written as imaginary-time propagation over intervals \(s\beta\hbar\) and \((1-s)\beta\hbar\). The trace closes the total interval \(\beta\hbar\), and the two operator insertions divide it into those two segments. At \(s=1/2\), both segments have equal length. This is what “halfway around” describes: a location in the thermal operator product, not a particle waiting for half a real-time period.

A more concrete representation is available at \(t=0\) for position observables. Insert two position resolutions into the midpoint position correlation:

\[ C_{1/2}^{qq}(0)=\operatorname{Tr}[\sqrt\rho\,q\sqrt\rho\,q] =\int du\,dv\;uv\,P(u,v), \qquad P(u,v)=|\langle u|\sqrt\rho|v\rangle|^2. \]

The kernel \(P\) is nonnegative and normalized because its integral is \(\operatorname{Tr}\rho=1\). Both marginals are the ordinary thermal position distribution. Nevertheless, \(u\) and \(v\) are two coordinates in this auxiliary thermal representation. They are not two sequential, undisturbed measurements of a particle following a real trajectory. The positive representation here is restricted to equal-time position multiplication operators; it does not provide a general real-time probability model for arbitrary quantum observables.

The centered pure-ground-state limit exposes the difference. With \(\rho=|0\rangle\langle0|\),

\[ P(u,v)=|\psi_0(u)|^2|\psi_0(v)|^2, \qquad C_{1/2}^{qq}(0)=\langle0|q|0\rangle^2=0, \]

although \(\langle0|q^2|0\rangle\) is nonzero. The two marginals each contain zero-point fluctuations, but the auxiliary coordinates factorize in this limit. A vanishing midpoint correlation therefore does not imply a vanishing position variance. Imaginary-time geometry is useful precisely when we keep this distinction visible.

5. The harmonic oscillator: the same frequencies, different weights

We can now compare all the correlations without any dynamical approximation. Take the one-dimensional oscillator and define its natural position and temperature scales:

\[ H=\frac{p^2}{2m}+\frac12m\Omega^2q^2 =\hbar\Omega\left(a^\dagger a+\frac12\right), \quad \ell^2=\frac{\hbar}{2m\Omega}, \quad x=\beta\hbar\Omega, \quad \theta=\Omega t, \quad \bar n=\frac{1}{e^x-1}. \]

The ground-state variance is \(\ell^2\). Larger \(x\) means lower temperature. Since \(q=\ell(a+a^\dagger)\), the position operator connects adjacent energy levels only. The Heisenberg operator is \(q(t)=\ell(ae^{-i\theta}+a^\dagger e^{i\theta})\). Using \(\langle aa^\dagger\rangle=\bar n+1\), \(\langle a^\dagger a\rangle=\bar n\), and the thermal ratio between adjacent levels gives

\[ \boxed{\frac{C_s^{qq}(t)}{\ell^2} =(\bar n+1)e^{-sx}e^{i\theta} +\bar n e^{sx}e^{-i\theta}.} \]

The two frequencies remain \(\pm\Omega\). Splitting the thermal state changes their weights, rather than replacing the quantum time evolution. Separating the real and imaginary parts yields

\[ \frac{C_s^{qq}(t)}{\ell^2} =\frac{\cosh[(1/2-s)x]}{\sinh(x/2)}\cos\theta +i\frac{\sinh[(1/2-s)x]}{\sinh(x/2)}\sin\theta. \]

From this single formula, the main conventions follow:

CorrelationExact position result
Ordered \(C_0\)\(\ell^2[\coth(x/2)\cos\theta+i\sin\theta]\)
Reverse ordered \(C_1\)\(\ell^2[\coth(x/2)\cos\theta-i\sin\theta]\)
Anticommutator symmetrized\(\ell^2\coth(x/2)\cos\theta\)
Midpoint \(C_{1/2}\)\(\ell^2\cos\theta/\sinh(x/2)\)
Kubo \(C_K\)\((2\ell^2/x)\cos\theta\)
Classical thermal\((\beta m\Omega^2)^{-1}\cos\theta\)

The Kubo integral is particularly instructive. Its two exponential coefficients satisfy

\[ (\bar n+1)\frac{1-e^{-x}}{x}=\frac1x, \qquad \bar n\frac{e^x-1}{x}=\frac1x. \]

Their sum gives \(2\cos\theta/x\). This equals the classical thermal position correlation exactly because \(2\ell^2/x=1/(\beta m\Omega^2)\). It is a property of this Hamiltonian and this linear observable, not a statement that the thermal oscillator is classical.

The imaginary part has a separate origin. From \(q(t)=q\cos\theta+p\sin\theta/(m\Omega)\) and \([q,p]=i\hbar\), the commutator gives \(\operatorname{Im}C_0=\ell^2\sin\theta\). It does not disappear at low temperature. Meanwhile, the midpoint amplitude tends to zero exponentially. The interactive comparison keeps the absolute amplitudes, so these differences are not hidden by normalizing each curve to its own initial value.

At low temperature, \(C_0^{qq}\to\ell^2e^{i\theta}\) and the symmetrized correlation tends to \(\ell^2\cos\theta\). The midpoint tends to zero because a ground-to-excited-state contribution is weighted by \(\sqrt{p_0p_1}\), and \(p_1\to0\). The ordered correlation instead retains the weight \(p_0\to1\). The Kubo correlation tends to zero as \(1/\beta\), but \(\beta C_K\) stays finite and retains the oscillator's linear response.

At high temperature, \(x\ll1\), all the position correlations have the same leading classical term. The ordered imaginary part remains finite at fixed \(\hbar\), but is small relative to the real amplitude of order \(1/x\). Leading classical agreement is therefore not exact equality at finite temperature.

The spectral difference is equally explicit:

\[ S_0^{qq}(\omega)=2\pi\ell^2[(\bar n+1)\delta(\omega-\Omega) +\bar n\delta(\omega+\Omega)], \qquad S_K^{qq}(\omega)=\frac{2\pi\ell^2}{x} [\delta(\omega-\Omega)+\delta(\omega+\Omega)]. \]

These are ideal delta lines, not peaks with a finite linewidth. Exact thermal reweighting recovers the asymmetric ordered weights from the symmetric Kubo weights at every finite \(x\). It cannot justify reading an untransformed Kubo line weight as the ordered one.

6. Position squared exposes the limit of the classical-looking result

The equality between the position Kubo and classical correlations invites an overgeneralization. We test it without changing the system: replace \(q\) by the fluctuation \(A=q^2-\langle q^2\rangle\). Each classical or quantum ensemble subtracts its own mean. This removes the disconnected mean product and leaves a comparison of genuine fluctuations.

The operator \(q^2=\ell^2[a^2+(a^\dagger)^2+2N+1]\), with \(N=a^\dagger a\), connects levels two steps apart and also has a diagonal component. Its connected correlation is

\[ \frac{C_s^{AA}(t)}{\ell^4} =4\bar n(\bar n+1) +2(\bar n+1)^2e^{-2sx}e^{2i\theta} +2\bar n^2e^{2sx}e^{-2i\theta}. \]

The constant term is the thermal variance of the conserved occupation number. Subtracting the mean does not remove it: an isolated oscillator retains this part of its initial fluctuation forever. The spectrum therefore contains a zero-frequency line as well as the two lines at \(\pm2\Omega\).

Integrating the two oscillatory terms over \(s\) gives

\[ \boxed{\frac{C_K^{AA}(t)}{\ell^4} =4\bar n(\bar n+1)+\frac{2}{x}\coth(x/2)\cos(2\theta).} \]

For comparison, a classical thermal oscillator has Gaussian position variance \(v=1/(\beta m\Omega^2)=2\ell^2/x\). Gaussian factorization gives \(\langle\delta q^2(0)\delta q^2(t)\rangle=2v^2\cos^2\theta\), hence

\[ \boxed{\frac{C_{\mathrm{cl}}^{AA}(t)}{\ell^4} =\frac{4}{x^2}[1+\cos(2\theta)].} \]

The two formulas generally differ. At \(x=6\), dividing their equal-time values by \(\ell^4\) gives approximately 0.345 and 0.222. The discrepancy remains even though the q–q Kubo result is exactly classical. Restoring \(\langle q^2\rangle^2\) in the quantum expression agrees with Hele's unconnected harmonic-oscillator formula, Eq. (27) [2].

There is a compact explanation of why the linear equality cannot be carried through the square. The oscillator is Gaussian, and the connected formulas satisfy

\[ C_s^{AA}(t)=2[C_s^{qq}(t)]^2, \qquad C_K^{AA}(t)=2\int_0^1ds\,[C_s^{qq}(t)]^2 \ne 2\left[\int_0^1ds\,C_s^{qq}(t)\right]^2. \]

Kubo averaging and taking the square do not commute. The midpoint and symmetrized nonlinear correlations also remain distinct: the former is \(4\ell^4\bar n(\bar n+1)[1+\cos(2\theta)]\), whereas the latter retains a low-temperature oscillation of amplitude \(2\ell^4\). This controlled counterexample is enough to reject a universal classical interpretation of Kubo correlations.

7. Why does an electronic transition require ordered weights?

The exact spectral conversion leaves a physical question unanswered: why does the transition formula contain the ordered correlation in the first place? It follows from a transition amplitude multiplied by its complex conjugate, with the bath's initial probabilities retained. The Kubo function is a valid quantum correlation, but its imaginary-time average uses different weights. We derive both statements for the same model.

Let the electronic states be \(|i\rangle\) and \(|f\rangle\), with energy change \(\Delta=E_f-E_i\). The bath has a Hamiltonian \(H_B\), shared by these electronic states in this simplified model, and a Hermitian coupling operator \(B\). The interaction is

\[ V=g|f\rangle\langle i|\otimes B +g^*|i\rangle\langle f|\otimes B. \]

The electronic matrix element is included in \(g\). Initially the electron is in \(|i\rangle\) and the bath is in its Gibbs state. All indices \(m,n\) below label bath states, not electronic states:

\[ H_B|m\rangle=\epsilon_m|m\rangle,\qquad \rho_B=\sum_m p_m|m\rangle\langle m|,\qquad p_m=\frac{e^{-\beta\epsilon_m}}{Z_B},\qquad B_{nm}=\langle n|B|m\rangle. \]

From a thermal trace to bath transitions

Open-system derivations often use the product with the later operator on the left. To keep the earlier sections and interactive figure unchanged, call this function \(D_B\), and retain \(C_B\) for the article's original order:

\[ D_B(t)=\langle B(t)B(0)\rangle,\qquad C_B(t)=\langle B(0)B(t)\rangle =D_B(-t)=D_B(t)^*. \]

Both are fixed-order, unsymmetrized correlations; neither symbol denotes time ordering. What is often called \(C(t)\) in an open-system calculation is our \(D_B(t)\). Insert a complete bath basis between its operators:

\[ \begin{aligned} D_B(t)&=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho_B B(t)B]\\ &=\sum_m p_m\langle m|B(t)B|m\rangle\\ &=\sum_{mn}p_m\langle m|B(t)|n\rangle B_{nm}. \end{aligned} \]

The Heisenberg evolution and Hermiticity supply the remaining two steps:

\[ \begin{aligned} \langle m|B(t)|n\rangle &=e^{i(\epsilon_m-\epsilon_n)t/\hbar}B_{mn},\\ B_{mn}B_{nm}&=|B_{nm}|^2. \end{aligned} \]

Thus the exact thermal trace is a sum over energy-resolved bath transitions:

\[ \boxed{D_B(t)=\sum_{mn}p_m|B_{nm}|^2 e^{-i(\epsilon_n-\epsilon_m)t/\hbar}.} \]

Each term has an initial occupation \(p_m\), a squared transition matrix element, and the phase of the bath energy change. Reading the rightmost operator first explains the matrix-element indices; the trace itself is not a sequence of two projective measurements. Reversing operator order changes the phase sign in the corresponding sum. The spectral conventions must change together:

\[ S_B(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\, e^{-i\omega t}C_B(t) =\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\, e^{i\omega t}D_B(t). \]

Consequently the article's convention and the common later-operator-first convention give the same \(S_B\). Positive frequency means an increase in bath energy in both conventions.

The initial occupation comes from the probability calculation

For a specified initial bath state \(m\) and final bath state \(n\), first-order interaction-picture perturbation theory gives

\[ A_{fn\leftarrow im}^{(1)}(t) =-\frac{igB_{nm}}{\hbar}\int_0^t d\tau\, e^{i[\Delta+\epsilon_n-\epsilon_m]\tau/\hbar}. \]

The bath is an incoherent thermal mixture, and its final state is unobserved. We must square each amplitude, average over initial \(m\) with probability \(p_m\), and sum over final \(n\). The leading nonzero transition probability is therefore

\[ P_{i\to f}^{(2)}(t)=\frac{|g|^2}{\hbar^2} \sum_{mn}p_m|B_{nm}|^2 \left|\int_0^t d\tau\, e^{i[\Delta+\epsilon_n-\epsilon_m]\tau/\hbar}\right|^2. \]

This calculation produces exactly the weights in \(D_B\). Equivalently, expanding the squared integral and using the spectral sum above gives the probability directly from the correlation:

\[ P_{i\to f}^{(2)}(t)=\frac{|g|^2}{\hbar^2} \int_0^t d\tau\int_0^t d\tau'\, e^{i\Delta(\tau-\tau')/\hbar}D_B(\tau'-\tau). \]

The two time arguments arise from the amplitude and its conjugate. In a reduced electronic equation, the same bath correlations weight past electronic operators or density matrices: this is how the bath enters through a memory kernel rather than through one prescribed real coordinate path. Here the formula is a second-order probability, not an exact all-orders electronic propagator.

The electronic phase selects bath energy changes that balance the gap. In a weak-coupling continuum with an appropriate constant-rate limit, the squared time integral per unit time becomes an energy delta function:

\[ k_{i\to f}=\frac{2\pi|g|^2}{\hbar} \sum_{mn}p_m|B_{nm}|^2\delta(\Delta+\epsilon_n-\epsilon_m) =\boxed{\frac{|g|^2}{\hbar^2}S_B(-\Delta/\hbar).} \]

An upward electronic transition samples negative bath frequency because the bath must supply energy; a downward electronic transition samples positive frequency because the bath receives energy. This follows from the amplitude calculation, rather than from choosing a name for a correlation. It agrees with the unsymmetrized-noise derivation of Clerk and collaborators [3].

Where the Kubo weight differs

Now do the same bath-basis expansion for the Kubo autocorrelation, written with the later operator first inside the thermal split. For a Hermitian autocorrelation its splitting integral equals the earlier \(C_K\):

\[ \begin{aligned} K_B(t)&=\frac{1}{\beta Z_B}\int_0^\beta d\lambda\, \operatorname{Tr}[e^{-(\beta-\lambda)H_B} B(t)e^{-\lambda H_B}B]\\ &=\sum_{mn}p_m|B_{nm}|^2e^{-i(\epsilon_n-\epsilon_m)t/\hbar} \frac1\beta\int_0^\beta d\lambda\, e^{-\lambda(\epsilon_n-\epsilon_m)}. \end{aligned} \]

The extra factor is an imaginary-time average, with \(\lambda\) measured in inverse-energy units. Evaluating it and using the Gibbs relation yields

\[ \frac1\beta\int_0^\beta d\lambda\,e^{-\lambda\delta\epsilon} =\frac{1-e^{-\beta\delta\epsilon}}{\beta\delta\epsilon}, \qquad p_n=p_m e^{-\beta(\epsilon_n-\epsilon_m)}, \]
\[ \boxed{K_B(t)=\sum_{mn}w_{mn}^{K}|B_{nm}|^2 e^{-i(\epsilon_n-\epsilon_m)t/\hbar},\qquad w_{mn}^{K}=\frac{p_m-p_n}{\beta(\epsilon_n-\epsilon_m)}.} \]

For zero energy difference the weight is defined by its continuous limit \(p_m\). Unlike the ordered initial probability, the Kubo weight is unchanged when the two bath states are exchanged:

Bath transitionOrdered weightKubo weight
\(m\to n\)\(p_m|B_{nm}|^2\)\(w_{mn}^{K}|B_{nm}|^2\)
\(n\to m\)\(p_n|B_{nm}|^2\)\(w_{mn}^{K}|B_{nm}|^2\)

At low temperature, an excited bath state has a small initial probability. The ordered correlation preserves that suppression of energy release by the bath. Kubo averaging assigns the two directions the same pair weight, a logarithmic mean rather than an arithmetic mean. Thus an unmodified replacement of \(D_B\) by \(K_B\) in the probability or rate formula changes the physics.

A two-level example makes the size of this change explicit. Take \(\beta\delta\epsilon=2\), where \(\delta\epsilon\) is the bath energy gap, and let the off-diagonal coupling matrix element be one. The initial probabilities are 0.880797 and 0.119203, while both Kubo line weights are 0.380797. The ordered ratio is \(e^{-2}\); the raw Kubo ratio is 1. The linked finite-matrix check verifies these weights, the operator trace, imaginary-time quadrature, and the finite-time probability identity without taking a rate limit.

The two-point information is nevertheless recoverable. Applying the spectral relation from Section 3 with the convention bridge above gives

\[ \widetilde D_B(\omega) =\frac{\beta\hbar\omega}{1-e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}} \widetilde K_B(\omega),\qquad \widetilde D_B(\omega)=\int dt\,e^{i\omega t}D_B(t). \]

At known finite temperature this recovers the two separate initial-state weights. Kubo is therefore not an invalid correlation or a quantum correlation that has lost detailed-balance information. It needs the appropriate conversion before insertion into a formula derived for ordered noise; a formula consistently rewritten in terms of Kubo data is equally legitimate. Linear response already provides such an example in Section 3.

The conversion requires thermal equilibrium, but does not require a harmonic bath. Separately, the two-point correlation suffices for the second-order expression derived here. For general finite coupling to a non-Gaussian bath, higher connected correlations can matter. An isolated oscillator or two-level bath also has recurrences rather than a generic irreversible exponential rate. State-dependent nuclear Hamiltonians require a correlation involving both nuclear propagators, and a non-Hermitian coupling requires its correct dagger/order pair.

8. Why a real bead trajectory is not automatically ordered quantum noise

The previous result makes the approximation issue precise. Standard position RPMD uses an equilibrium ring-polymer ensemble and approximate real-time propagation to estimate Kubo correlations, as in the existing RPMD estimator derivation. A single bead coordinate is a different observable from the bead-averaged estimator, and fictitious internal modes can appear in its real-time signal.

For a harmonic well with P beads and standard physical bead masses, the ring-polymer mode frequencies are

\[ \Omega_k^{\mathrm{RP}}= \sqrt{\Omega^2+4\omega_P^2\sin^2(\pi k/P)}, \qquad \omega_P=\frac{P}{\beta\hbar}, \quad k=0,\ldots,P-1. \]

The centroid selects \(k=0\), which oscillates at the physical frequency \(\Omega\). A single bead contains internal modes as well. Recovering the quantum equal-time position distribution does not make these internal frequencies physical bath transitions. Increasing P improves imaginary-time discretization; it does not by itself turn the bead's real-time trajectory into an exact quantum trajectory.

There is a second independent issue. A stationary real scalar signal \(f(t)\) has an even autocorrelation because stationarity and commutativity give

\[ \langle f(0)f(-t)\rangle=\langle f(t)f(0)\rangle =\langle f(0)f(t)\rangle. \]

Its autocorrelation spectrum is symmetric. If an electron is driven by such externally prescribed noise, keeping a complex electronic amplitude supplies the oscillating phase in the perturbative formula, but does not manufacture the bath's missing positive/negative-frequency asymmetry.

This supports a specific criticism: the standard RPMD Kubo interpretation does not justify treating an individual real bead path as ordered quantum bath noise. It does not establish that every electronic method using a ring polymer is wrong. Nuclear feedback, state-dependent sampling, transition rules, or an explicitly derived spectral correction can change the problem and must be examined on their own terms. Even a correct conversion of an approximate Kubo spectrum leaves its dynamical approximation error.

The useful test is therefore to identify the bath operator, thermal preparation, real-time generator, estimator, and rate or response formula in the actual method. A matching equilibrium distribution and a quantum electronic phase solve different parts of that test. Neither substitutes for the required dynamical correlation.

From a sampled Kubo function to electronic propagation

The distinction leads to a concrete workflow. First fix the reference bath Hamiltonian, equilibrium preparation, and physical coupling operator. For a nonzero mean coupling, include that mean in the electronic Hamiltonian and use the centered bath operator for the fluctuation correlation. Then estimate \(K_B(t)\) from equilibrium RPMD trajectories with the appropriate bead-averaged estimator, Fourier transform it, apply the exact thermal factor, and transform back to obtain an approximate ordered input \(D_B(t)\). Keep its real and imaginary parts: they encode the anticommutator fluctuations and commutator response with the chosen order.

A pole expansion of this complex input can turn the history integrals of a specified memory equation into coupled ordinary differential equations for the electronic density matrix and auxiliary matrices, often denoted \(\chi\). Those matrices store bath memory; they do not represent additional physical bath coordinates. This localization does not remove the approximation in the memory equation. In particular, a second-order closure remains a second-order closure, and extra auxiliary variables cannot reconstruct omitted higher connected bath correlations.

There are three separate accuracy questions: how well RPMD estimates the true Kubo dynamics, how well the spectral or pole representation reproduces that input over the relevant frequencies and times, and how well the chosen reduced electronic equation applies. The thermal conversion is exact for exact equilibrium data, including an anharmonic bath. It cannot repair an inaccurate RPMD frequency, amplitude, or decay. Harmonic dynamics with a linear position coupling is a special exact Kubo benchmark; it is not a general accuracy guarantee for anharmonic couplings or baths.

The two-level transition check uses \(\hbar=1\), bath energies 0 and 1, \(\beta=2\), and \(B=\sigma_x\). It compares the matrix trace with the spectral sum and 64-point Gauss–Legendre imaginary-time quadrature. It also compares the amplitude-squared probability with direct double-time quadrature for upward, downward, and detuned electronic gaps, using both 32 and 64 quadrature points. These are algebra and quadrature checks of the stated second-order expression, not a comparison with exact coupled electron–bath dynamics.

  • sho_correlations.py: independent energy-basis trace and Kubo quadrature checks; requires NumPy.
  • Numerical check results: parameters, basis size, and error definition.
  • transition_weights.py and its check results: two-level ordered/Kubo weights, spectral recovery, and finite-time electronic probability; requires NumPy.
  • sho-correlations.js and figure styles: formula-driven real-time, thermal-split, and spectral-line explorer.

For known finite-temperature equilibrium, a complete exact Kubo correlation does determine the corresponding ordered two-point correlation. The two functions nevertheless have different values and enter observable formulas through different operations. The harmonic oscillator shows both facts at once; its nonlinear q² fluctuation also shows why a classical-looking Kubo result for one observable cannot be promoted to a general statement about quantum dynamics.
