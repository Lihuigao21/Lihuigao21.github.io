MMST Mapping and ZPE Leakage

2026.05.29 - Mapping Dynamics / Zero-Point Energy / SQC

The previous MQC notes compared active-surface dynamics, mean-field dynamics, and a directed density-matrix correction. This part turns to the Meyer-Miller-Stock-Thoss mapping Hamiltonian. MMST is attractive because it gives discrete electronic states continuous classical variables, but that same enlargement of phase space creates one of its central failure modes: electronic zero-point-energy leakage.

Clarification in Part VI. The parameter in this article is the legacy offset inside the factor \(1/2\); it equals twice the CPS offset used in Part VI. Negative single-trajectory raw actions are symbol excursions, and do not alone establish inaccurate dynamics or nuclear energy transfer. Part VI derives exact operator reconstruction and signed kernels; Part VII examines errors introduced by classical nuclear feedback. Why Mapping Is Needed

Mixed quantum-classical dynamics needs a closure. The nuclei require forces, while the electronic subsystem is intrinsically discrete. Ehrenfest dynamics closes the problem with a density-matrix mean force. FSSH closes it with one active adiabatic surface and stochastic hops. MMST takes a different route: it first rewrites the discrete electronic Hilbert space in terms of continuous oscillator coordinates, then propagates those variables together with the nuclei.

This is not merely a convenient analogy. The Stock-Thoss construction maps an \(F\)-state electronic subsystem to \(F\) harmonic-oscillator degrees of freedom and is exact if the oscillator problem is treated quantum mechanically in the singly-excited oscillator subspace. The approximation enters when the oscillator variables are treated as classical coordinates and momenta. That distinction is important: the mapping itself is not the weak link; the classical use of the enlarged mapping phase space is.

The physical picture is simple. A discrete electronic state is replaced by a fictitious oscillator excitation: state \(i\) means that oscillator \(i\) carries one quantum while all other electronic oscillators remain in their ground levels. Electronic populations become oscillator actions, and electronic coherences become relative phases between oscillator coordinates. Diagonal electronic energies weight the oscillator actions, while off-diagonal couplings mix oscillator modes and transfer action between them.

MMST Hamiltonian

Start from an electronic Hamiltonian in a diabatic basis,

\[ \hat{H} = \frac{P^2}{2M} + \hat{h}(R), \qquad \hat{h}(R) = \sum_{ij} h_{ij}(R)\,|i\rangle\langle j|. \]

The Stock-Thoss mapping embeds the electronic basis into a singly-excited oscillator, or SEO, subspace:

\[ |i\rangle \longrightarrow |0_1,\ldots,1_i,\ldots,0_F\rangle. \]

Within that SEO subspace, the electronic transition operator is represented by a bosonic number-transfer operator,

\[ |i\rangle\langle j| \longrightarrow \hat{a}_i^\dagger \hat{a}_j, \]

so the exact quantum mapping Hamiltonian is

\[ \hat{H}_{\mathrm{map}} = \frac{P^2}{2M} + \sum_{ij} h_{ij}(R)\,\hat{a}_i^\dagger\hat{a}_j. \]

Using dimensionless oscillator variables,

\[ \hat{a}_i = \frac{\hat{x}_i+i\hat{p}_i}{\sqrt{2}}, \qquad \hat{a}_i^\dagger = \frac{\hat{x}_i-i\hat{p}_i}{\sqrt{2}}, \]

the symmetrized classical symbol of \(\hat{a}_i^\dagger\hat{a}_j\) gives the real mapping bilinear. For a real symmetric diabatic Hamiltonian, the antisymmetric imaginary terms cancel in the full \(ij\) sum, leaving

\[ \hat{a}_i^\dagger\hat{a}_j \rightsquigarrow \frac{1}{2} \left(x_i x_j+p_i p_j-\delta_{ij}\right). \]

This \(-\delta_{ij}\) term is the oscillator zero-point subtraction that appears when the quantum number operator is written in coordinate and momentum variables. Many quasiclassical mapping variants replace it by an adjustable \(-\gamma\delta_{ij}\) term. The direct MMST choice is \(\gamma=1\); reduced-\(\gamma\), SQC, and related variants use this offset as a controlled way to change the electronic zero-point-energy content of the mapping phase space.

Replacing the oscillator operators by classical coordinates and momenta then gives the basic MMST Hamiltonian,

\[ H_{\mathrm{MMST}} = \frac{P^2}{2M} + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{ij} h_{ij}(R) \left(x_i x_j+p_i p_j-\gamma\delta_{ij}\right). \]

Each electronic state \(i\) has one mapping pair \((x_i,p_i)\). It is often useful to combine them into a complex mapping amplitude,

\[ z_i=\frac{x_i+i p_i}{\sqrt{2}}, \qquad \rho_{ij}^{\mathrm{map}} = z_i z_j^* - \frac{\gamma}{2}\delta_{ij}. \]

The diagonal part gives the raw mapping population, or action estimator,

\[ n_i = \rho_{ii}^{\mathrm{map}} = \frac{1}{2}\left(x_i^2+p_i^2-\gamma\right). \]

The nuclei move on the gradient of the mapping Hamiltonian. In a compact notation, the electronic variables feed back through a mean-field-like force,

\[ F_R = -\frac{\partial H_{\mathrm{MMST}}}{\partial R} = - \sum_{ij} \rho_{ij}^{\mathrm{map}} \frac{\partial h_{ij}(R)}{\partial R}. \]

This is why a basic MMST trajectory resembles Ehrenfest more than FSSH: the nuclei usually do not run on a single active surface. They feel a continuous force determined by the instantaneous mapping density.

Electronic Initialization

The simplest MMST initialization is a focused pure-state initialization. If the trajectory starts in electronic state \(k\), the electronic actions are set to

\[ n_k=1, \qquad n_{i\ne k}=0. \]

The mapping radii follow from the action definition,

\[ r_i=\sqrt{2n_i+\gamma}, \qquad x_i=r_i\cos\theta_i, \qquad p_i=r_i\sin\theta_i. \]

The phases \(\theta_i\) are sampled uniformly. This means that even with a fixed nuclear initial condition, MMST is still an ensemble method: different electronic phases give different mapping coherences and, once nuclear back reaction is included, different nuclear forces. In the Tully SAC comparison below, MMST is therefore averaged over 64 electronic phase samples.

Negative \(\gamma\) requires more care. For an inactive state, focused initialization asks for \(r_i=\sqrt{\gamma}\). If \(\gamma<0\), that radius is not real. The local implementation therefore allows negative \(\gamma\) only with explicit mapping coordinates or a constrained/windowed sampler, rather than with the default focused pure-state initializer.

The average MMST population is smooth and finite, and the scattering outcome only reports an ensemble observable. To expose leakage, one needs to look inside the mapping variables themselves.

What ZPE Leakage Means

Quantum mechanically, the electronic mapping oscillators should remain in the singly-excited oscillator subspace. Classically, a trajectory can explore regions of the oscillator phase space that have no direct electronic-state interpretation. The raw action estimator can then become negative or larger than one:

\[ n_i<0 \quad\mathrm{or}\quad n_i>1. \]

These raw-action excursions are often used as a ZPE-leakage diagnostic. They are not by themselves a proof of dynamical error: exact phase-space reconstruction can include signed symbols. If the same raw mapping kernel also enters an approximate nuclear force, however, those weights can affect the trajectory before any post-processing correction is applied. An inaccurate observable or energy exchange must be assessed against the appropriate reference.

A minimal two-state example makes the issue transparent. Consider a constant energy gap and a constant derivative coupling on a prescribed constant-velocity nuclear path. Nuclear backreaction is switched off in this diagnostic, leaving a clean two-level electronic precession. Constant adiabatic energies alone would not make the full coupled nuclear force vanish when derivative couplings are present. For two states, the raw action obeys

\[ n_i=|z_i|^2-\frac{\gamma}{2}, \qquad |z_0|^2+|z_1|^2=1+\gamma. \]

Therefore a single raw population can range over

\[ -\frac{\gamma}{2} \le n_i \le 1+\frac{\gamma}{2}. \]

The leakage interval is built into the classical mapping geometry. With \(\gamma=1\), a single trajectory can reach almost \([-0.5,1.5]\). With \(\gamma=1/3\), the interval shrinks to approximately \([-1/6,7/6]\). With \(\gamma=0\), this particular pure electronic diagnostic has no raw action outside \([0,1]\), but that limit also removes the electronic zero-point offset that motivated the MMST phase-space broadening in the first place.

Correction Strategies

The literature does not contain one universal fix. Different corrections target different layers of the problem: the phase-space offset, the population estimator, the initial force, the nuclear quantum distribution, or the numerical integrator.

Reduced Gamma

The simplest idea is to reduce \(\gamma\). Since the raw action interval scales as \([-\gamma/2,1+\gamma/2]\), smaller positive \(\gamma\) directly shrinks the leakage range. Mueller and Stock treated this as a reduced ZPE correction and connected the choice of \(\gamma\) to level-density criteria. The drawback is that a smaller \(\gamma\) changes the mapping phase-space distribution and can move the method toward a classical-path or Ehrenfest-like limit.

Negative Gamma

He, Gong, Wu, and Liu pointed out that the mapping constraint permits negative \(\gamma\), provided

\[ \gamma>-\frac{2}{F} \]

for an \(F\)-state electronic subsystem in this article's legacy convention. In a two-state model, \(\gamma>-1\). The published CPS convention instead has the bound \(\gamma_{\mathrm{CPS}}>-1/F\), with \(\gamma=2\gamma_{\mathrm{CPS}}\). Negative \(\gamma\) shrinks the raw-action interval from the other side; for example, the legacy value \(\gamma=-0.2\) gives \([0.1,0.9]\). This bounds the raw kernel, not the final dual-readout population. Negative CPS parameters can improve some spin-boson benchmarks, but they are not a drop-in focused initialization because inactive states would require imaginary radii unless a different sampler is used.

Clipping and Renormalization

One can post-process the raw action by clipping negative values and renormalizing. This produces a physical-looking population trace, but it is only an estimator correction. It does not change the nuclear force that was already generated by the uncorrected mapping density.

SQC Windowing

The symmetrical quasiclassical approach replaces direct use of raw actions by window functions. Instead of reading \(n_i\) as a continuous population, a trajectory is assigned to a state when its action falls inside that state's window. Square, triangle, and generalized triangle windows are common. Windowing is a practical re-quantization of the electronic oscillator variables and is often much better behaved than the raw action estimator, but it introduces choices: window shape, width, initial sampling, final binning, and the treatment of unassigned trajectories.

Trajectory-Adjusted Gamma

SQC sampling gives each trajectory a slightly different electronic oscillator energy. On steep repulsive surfaces, this can make some trajectories feel an initial nuclear force that does not match the intended electronic state. Cotton and Miller proposed keeping the SQC-sampled \((x,p)\) distribution but adjusting the \(\gamma\)-parameter per trajectory so that the initial nuclear force corresponds exactly to the prepared electronic state. This is not meant to solve every long-time equilibrium issue, but it directly fixes a short-time force inconsistency that can otherwise create spurious trajectories.

Spin Mapping and Ring-Polymer Variants

Spin mapping uses a fixed-radius finite-state geometry and preserves the normalization structure naturally. Its W kernel can still have negative eigenvalues, so this does not universally eliminate inaccurate energy exchange from classical nuclear feedback. Part V develops spin mapping and its propagator; Part VII explains its relation to scalar CPS and MMST. Ring-polymer mapping extensions address the separate issue of nuclear quantum statistics.

MMST Workflow Used in This Benchmark

The population panels are generated directly by mmst_correction_comparison.py. They use MMST mapping variables on one prescribed nuclear path, with exact electronic propagation. They do not call the coupled MMST class used in the earlier SAC calculation. Here \(\gamma\) retains this article's oscillator convention: the subtracted action is \(\gamma/2\), so \(\gamma=2\gamma_{\mathrm{CPS}}\) in Part VI.

  1. Prepare a focused electronic ensemble. Start in the lower adiabatic state, \(\rho_0=|0\rangle\langle0|\). For each of 512 samples, draw two independent uniform phases with NumPy seed 2468 and set

    \[ \begin{gathered} z_i^{(a)}(0)=\sqrt{\delta_{i0}+\gamma/2}\,e^{i\theta_i^{(a)}},\\ \theta_i^{(a)}\sim\mathcal U[0,2\pi). \end{gathered} \]

    Thus every sample initially has \(n_0=1\), \(n_1=0\), and total oscillator action \(\sum_i|z_i|^2=1+\gamma\). The same phases are reused for \(\gamma=1,1/3,0\). This samples phases at fixed radii on the constraint sphere; it is neither full-space Wigner sampling nor uniform sampling of the entire CPS sphere.

  2. Prescribe the nuclear path and propagate the mapping variables. Use atomic units, \(M=2000\), \(P_0=200\), constant velocity \(v=0.1\), energy gap \(\Delta=0.002\), and derivative coupling \(d_{01}=0.05\). No nuclear force or momentum update is evaluated. The constant moving-basis electronic generator and its solution are

    \[ \begin{gathered} H_{\mathrm{eff}}= \begin{pmatrix}0&-i v d_{01}\\i v d_{01}&\Delta\end{pmatrix},\\ \dot z=-iH_{\mathrm{eff}}z,\\ z(t)=U(t)z(0),\qquad U(t)=e^{-iH_{\mathrm{eff}}t}. \end{gathered} \]

    The script diagonalizes this Hermitian matrix once and evaluates its exponential at 1001 times from 0 to 2500. Its --dt 2.5 is the output interval, not an RK4 or nuclear integration step. Unitarity conserves each sample's total action without clipping or norm correction.

  3. Read out the raw action and average with equal weights. In the same adiabatic basis used for propagation, the raw upper-state estimator is

    \[ \begin{gathered} n_1^{(a)}(t)=|z_1^{(a)}(t)|^2-\gamma/2,\\ \overline n_1(t)=\frac{1}{512}\sum_{a=1}^{512}n_1^{(a)}(t). \end{gathered} \]

    The black reference propagates the physical initial vector \(c(0)=(1,0)^{\mathsf T}\) with the same \(U(t)\) and reads \(|c_1(t)|^2\). No CPS dual kernel or initial signed weight is used in this focused protocol. Instead, its continuous phase average satisfies

    \[ \begin{gathered} \mathbb E_\theta\!\left[z(0)z(0)^\dagger-\frac{\gamma}{2}I\right]=\rho_0,\\ \mathbb E_\theta\!\left[z(t)z(t)^\dagger-\frac{\gamma}{2}I\right] =U(t)\rho_0U(t)^\dagger. \end{gathered} \]

    Consequently, raw-action averaging is electronically exact in the infinite-phase limit for every nonnegative \(\gamma\) here. The raw RMS errors 0.0156 and 0.0079 are finite-phase sampling errors in this model. At \(\gamma=0\), the only sampled phase is an irrelevant overall phase, so each sample gives the exact population. This fixed-path result does not establish accuracy after nuclear feedback is restored, as discussed in Part VII.

  4. Apply the alternative estimators to those same trajectories. Clipping replaces \(n_i\) by \(\max(n_i,0)\) and normalizes each sample before averaging. Window/bin assigns the state with the larger raw action. The curve labelled “SQC square” in the figure uses the implemented final filter

    \[ \begin{gathered} W_i(n)=\mathbf 1\{n_i\ge1-w,\ n_{1-i}\le w\},\\ w=1/3,\\ P_1^{\mathrm{win}}(t)= \frac{\sum_a W_1(n^{(a)}(t))} {\sum_a[W_0(n^{(a)}(t))+W_1(n^{(a)}(t))]}. \end{gathered} \]

    Since \(n_0+n_1=1\), samples with both actions between \(1/3\) and \(2/3\) remain unassigned. The denominator is the assigned count at each time, rather than 512; the code returns zero if that count vanishes. This simplified filter has no finite outer window bounds and is paired with focused initial radii, not initial square-window sampling. It is an estimator diagnostic, not a benchmark of a complete SQC method. None of these readout changes affects the propagated variables.

For each estimator, RMS error is computed over all 1001 output times. Raw minima and maxima cover every state, phase sample, and output time. The outside fraction counts time/sample pairs with at least one raw action outside \([0,1]\), using a \(10^{-12}\) tolerance; assigned averages window assignment over the same pairs. The negative-\(\gamma\) panel only evaluates the analytic bounds \([-\gamma/2,1+\gamma/2]\), subject to \(\gamma>-1\). No negative-\(\gamma\) trajectories are propagated.

Separate Workflow for the Initial-Force Panel

The lower-right panel does not continue the electronic trajectories above. It draws 20,000 independent pairs of oscillator actions with seed 62468, a fixed \(\gamma=1/3\), and uniform fluctuations \(\xi_i\in[-0.25,0.25]\):

\[ J_i^{(a)}=\max\!\left(\delta_{i0}+\frac{\gamma}{2}+\xi_i^{(a)},\,10^{-12}\right). \]

Here \(J_i=|z_i|^2\) is oscillator action, not a physical electronic energy. These SQC-like samples need not have a fixed total action. With diagonal state forces \(F_0=-1\) and \(F_1=2\), the target initial force is \(F_0\). The two protocols compare

\[ \begin{gathered} F_{\mathrm{fixed}}^{(a)}=\sum_i\left(J_i^{(a)}-\frac{\gamma}{2}\right)F_i,\\ \gamma_i^{(a)}=2\left(J_i^{(a)}-\delta_{i0}\right),\\ F_{\mathrm{adjusted}}^{(a)}=\sum_i\left(J_i^{(a)}-\frac{\gamma_i^{(a)}}{2}\right)F_i=F_0. \end{gathered} \]

The adjusted offset is different for each state and each sample. It makes the initial diagonal force exact by construction; the fixed-offset force-error standard deviation is 0.3064. No electronic phases, off-diagonal force, nuclear propagation, or long-time relaxation are tested in this panel. Together, the panels illustrate action excursions, estimator bias, and an initial-force correction; they do not measure energy transferred to nuclei.

The linked script requires only NumPy and Matplotlib. Run it from the website repository without installing the toymodel package:

python assets/code/mqc/mmst_correction_comparison.py --output-dir output/mmst-correction

Its default parameters reproduce the published summary table. The SAC figure earlier in the article instead uses the coupled MMST implementation, where the mapping density determines the nuclear force.

What Should Be Implemented Next?

The current toymodel implementation should be read as a baseline fixed-\(\gamma\) MMST method. It is useful for testing the mapping Hamiltonian, phase averaging, raw-action leakage, and comparisons against FSSH, Ehrenfest, and DVR. It is not yet a complete SQC/MMST implementation.

The following list records the development directions proposed when this baseline note was written; spin mapping is now treated in Part V, and CPS theory and dynamics in Part VI and Part VII:

  • SQC_MMST with square and triangle windows, including initial window sampling and final binning;
  • a constrained sampler for negative \(\gamma\), especially for spin-boson relaxation benchmarks;
  • a trajectory-adjusted \(\gamma\) option for SQC initial conditions on steep surfaces;
  • a split-Liouvillian or momentum-integral integrator for better long-time Hamiltonian stability;
  • a spin-mapping or spin-PLDM-lite comparison, so the site can compare oscillator mapping with fixed-radius spin mapping.

A good diagnostic protocol should report more than the mean population. For every MMST-style calculation, the article should also record raw action extrema, outside-\([0,1]\) fraction, the estimator used for population, the value of \(\gamma\), the number of electronic phase samples, and any window assignment fraction.
