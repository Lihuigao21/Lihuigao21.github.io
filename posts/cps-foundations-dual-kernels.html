MQC Series - Part VI

CPS, Dual Kernels, and Classical Dynamics

2026.10.12 · Updated 2026.10.13 - CPS / MMST / Spin Mapping / Stratonovich-Weyl

0. Background I. FSSH II. Ehrenfest III. P-Matrix IV. MMST V. Spin Mapping VI. CPS

MMST replaces a finite electronic system by fictitious oscillators. CPS asks a more precise question: can the finite system itself be represented on a constrained phase space, with an explicit rule for recovering every operator? Answering that question requires a kernel, a measure, and a dual. It also exposes exactly where a classical trajectory approximation stops being quantum mechanics.

CPS means constraint coordinate–momentum phase space. This article has two chapters. The first starts from MMST and derives the scalar CPS representation. The second derives its propagation boundary and compares actual CPS, MMST, spin-mapping, and Ehrenfest calculations. The discussion follows the questions raised by their comparison, rather than surveying every CPS extension.

Conventions. Use an orthonormal, fixed diabatic basis with \(F\ge2\) states and set \(\hbar=1\). Nuclear variables are \((R,P)\); mapping variables are \((x_n,p_n)\). Define \(z_n=(x_n+ip_n)/\sqrt2\) and the dimensionless action \(J_n=|z_n|^2\). Here the diagonal offset is \(J_n-\gamma\). Parts IV and V write their parameter inside a factor one-half, so their \(\Gamma=2\gamma\). Ordinary oscillator MMST has \(\gamma=1/2\) in the convention used below.

Chapter 1 — From MMST to an Exact CPS Representation

1.1 What MMST Maps, and What Remains Unresolved

Start with the electronic potential operator \(V(R)=\sum_{nm}V_{nm}(R)|n\rangle\langle m|\). Stock–Thoss oscillator mapping identifies each electronic basis state with one excitation in its corresponding fictitious oscillator:

\[ |n\rangle\longleftrightarrow |0,\ldots,1_n,\ldots,0\rangle,\qquad |n\rangle\langle m|\longleftrightarrow \hat a_n^\dagger\hat a_m \quad\text{within the single-excitation subspace}. \]

The identity is exact on that subspace. The oscillator Hilbert space is larger, so projection and the operator representation matter. With \(\hat a_n=(\hat x_n+i\hat p_n)/\sqrt2\), the number operator is \(\hat a_n^\dagger\hat a_n=(\hat x_n^2+\hat p_n^2-1)/2\). Its Weyl symbol is \(J_n-1/2\). For a real symmetric potential matrix, the familiar scalar Hamiltonian is therefore

\[ H_{\mathrm{MMST}} =\frac{P^2}{2M} +\sum_{nm}V_{nm}(R) \left[\frac{x_nx_m+p_np_m}{2} -\frac12\delta_{nm}\right]. \]

This formula alone does not specify a simulation. One must still specify how the physical electronic state is represented initially, how the enlarged space is projected, what dynamics propagates the variables, and how populations are read out. Full Cartesian Wigner sampling, focused initialization, and windowed methods are different protocols.

The source of concern is already visible: an exact operator embedding does not make independent classical oscillator trajectories exact for a coupled electron–nuclear system. Nor does an individual number symbol have to remain a physical probability. These are questions about representation and propagation that should be separated before proposing a repair. The MMST transformation itself is not an intrinsically inaccurate representation. [1]

1.2 Why Liu and Collaborators Developed CPS

The published motivation has several stages. Liu's unified mapping framework sought to derive finite-state mapping Hamiltonians directly from operator relations, with the Meyer–Miller Hamiltonian as one member, rather than requiring the conventional single-excitation oscillator construction. He and Liu's 2019 work then explored nonadiabatic dynamics using a constrained electronic phase space and a physically constrained population construction. That paper explicitly recognized the quantum exactness of MMST. [2]

The 2021 formulation made the broader problem explicit: construct an invertible correspondence between finite electronic operators and phase-space symbols, combine a constrained electronic space with the continuous nuclear space, and derive practical trajectory approximations from that representation. It also showed why the scalar offset need not be positive and why it can be generalized to a commutator matrix. [3] [4]

The later CPS review emphasizes another practical motivation: retain a useful coordinate–momentum description whose electronic equations are linear, while treating a composite system with discrete electronic and continuous nuclear variables. This is a computationally convenient representation of the finite system, with an explicit inverse transformation. [5]

These are the authors' stated theoretical and algorithmic aims. Our teaching interpretation is to separate three questions: what represents the quantum operator, what recovers its expectation, and what approximation propagates it? CPS clarifies those questions. It does not establish that the original MMST mapping was wrong, or that every CPS trajectory method eliminates ZPE leakage.

1.3 Why Map Operators onto Phase Space?

A wavefunction alone is insufficient for this task. We need one representation for the initial density, the Hamiltonian, the force operator, and any final observable. Let \(K(u)\) be an operator-valued kernel labeled by a continuous phase-space point \(u\). Associate an electronic operator \(A\) with the scalar function

\[ a(u)=\operatorname{Tr}[A K(u)]. \]

For example, the population projector \(C_n=|n\rangle\langle n|\) gives a population symbol; \(V(R)\) gives a potential symbol; \(-\nabla_RV(R)\) gives a force symbol. A continuous symbol can be integrated by quadrature or sampling, and its Hamiltonian can motivate a trajectory approximation. To make the representation useful, however, these scalar functions must contain enough information to reconstruct \(A\).

The requirement is therefore stronger than reproducing one selected population. For every pair of electronic operators, we want a trace identity of the form

\[ \operatorname{Tr}(AB) =F\int d\mu(u)\, \operatorname{Tr}[A K(u)] \operatorname{Tr}[B\bar K(u)], \qquad \int d\mu=1. \]

The second kernel \(\bar K\) is the dual needed to make this identity hold. The factor \(F\) belongs to our normalized-measure convention; a measure of total volume \(F\) absorbs it. We will derive both the measure and the dual below.

Mapping supplies a different representation of the same information. It does not by itself improve accuracy, and it does not make operator multiplication ordinary multiplication of symbols. Also, the one-to-one correspondence is with the finite-dimensional space of allowed operator symbols, not with every possible function on the sphere.

1.4 Assumptions, Parameter Counting, and the Kernel

Why should a kernel have the familiar form \(zz^\dagger-\gamma I\), and why should there be exactly one parameter? Neither statement follows from the word “mapping.” We must first specify the construction. Consider the scalar, fixed-radius family with these requirements:

  1. A point labels one normalized complex direction \(u\), with \(u^\dagger u=1\); the common phase of \(u\) is redundant.
  2. The kernel is Hermitian, so Hermitian observables have real symbols.
  3. A change of electronic basis acts covariantly: \(K(Uu)=U K(u)U^\dagger\) for every \(U\in U(F)\).
  4. The identity has symbol one at every point: \(\operatorname{Tr}K(u)=1\).
  5. For the standard Cartesian CPS branch, the direction-dependent part is a positive rank-one outer product of canonical mapping amplitudes.

The third requirement prevents a preferred electronic basis from being hidden in the kernel. To use it, choose the reference direction \(u_0=e_1\). Any unitary that rotates the orthogonal subspace while preserving this ray has the block form \(U_0=\operatorname{diag}(e^{i\theta},W)\), with \(W\in U(F-1)\). Covariance demands \(U_0K(u_0)U_0^\dagger=K(u_0)\).

The independently variable phase removes the off-diagonal blocks between the reference direction and its orthogonal complement. Invariance under every \(W\) makes the remaining orthogonal block proportional to its identity. Hermiticity makes both eigenvalues real. Thus

\[ K(u_0)= \begin{pmatrix} \lambda_\parallel&0\\ 0&\lambda_\perp I_{F-1} \end{pmatrix}. \]

Rotate \(e_1\) to any \(u\), and write \(\Pi(u)=uu^\dagger\). The general covariant result in this construction class is

\[ K(u)=\lambda_\parallel\Pi +\lambda_\perp(I-\Pi) =a\Pi+bI,\qquad a=\lambda_\parallel-\lambda_\perp,\quad b=\lambda_\perp. \]

There are initially two real coefficients. Unit trace imposes \(a+Fb=1\), leaving one. At \(a=0\), the kernel is \(I/F\) everywhere and loses every traceless operator; an invertible representation excludes that point.

Covariance and unit trace alone allow either sign of nonzero \(a\). Standard scalar CPS makes the additional positive Cartesian choice in requirement 5: \(a=s>0\), \(z=\sqrt{s}\,u\), and \(b=-\gamma\). The trace condition now gives the radius, rather than postulating it independently:

\[ \begin{aligned} K_\gamma(z)&=zz^\dagger-\gamma I,\\ \operatorname{Tr}K_\gamma&=z^\dagger z-F\gamma=1,\\ z^\dagger z&=s=1+F\gamma,\qquad \gamma>-\frac1F. \end{aligned} \]

Equivalently, \(K_\gamma=s\Pi-\gamma I\). The radius is \(\sqrt{2s}\) in \((x,p)\) coordinates. Its coefficient of \(zz^\dagger\) is fixed by the chosen canonical convention; changing that coefficient while retaining the same coordinates and bracket would change the electronic equations. An abstract negative-\(a\) covariant kernel is possible, but it is not this positive-radius Cartesian construction.

Why not zero parameters? Normalization does not fix the relative scale of the traceless part. The duality condition will pair that scale with an inverse scale, leaving \(\gamma\) free. An extra requirement, such as self-duality, can remove the freedom. Why not two? Radius and offset are tied by unit trace. Two independently adjustable values violate that requirement in this ansatz. Matrix kernels, weighted radii, or different phase-space domains can introduce more choices; the whole CPS family is not universally a one-parameter theory.

1.5 What the Constrained Sphere Represents

The derived norm constraint puts \((x,p)\) on \(S^{2F-1}\). The kernel depends only on \(zz^\dagger\), so the common phase gives the same point for all physical symbols. The space of distinct directions is

\[ S^{2F-1}/U(1) =\mathbb{CP}^{F-1} =SU(F)/S\!\left(U(1)\times U(F-1)\right). \]

This quotient is the group-theory version of the stabilizer used in the derivation: \(SU(F)\) moves a reference ray to another ray, while transformations preserving it are redundant. There are \(2F-2\) real directional degrees of freedom. For \(F=2\), this is the ordinary Bloch sphere. For larger \(F\), it is a projective manifold, not the entire sphere of arbitrary generalized Bloch vectors.

The physical pure-state object is \(\Pi\), not necessarily \(K_\gamma\). Their distinction follows directly from the spectrum:

\[ \operatorname{spec}K_\gamma =\{1+(F-1)\gamma,-\gamma,\ldots,-\gamma\}. \]

Positive \(\gamma\) gives negative eigenvalues. That is admissible for a representation kernel; it is inadmissible for a physical density matrix. At nonpositive allowed \(\gamma\), the kernel is positive, but positivity of the force kernel will not make the dual a positive readout.

To connect with spin variables, use traceless Hermitian generators \(T_a\), \(a=1,\ldots,F^2-1\), normalized by \(\operatorname{Tr}(T_aT_b)=\delta_{ab}/2\). Then

\[ \Pi=\frac IF+\sum_a q_aT_a,\qquad q_a=2\operatorname{Tr}(\Pi T_a),\qquad K_\gamma=\frac IF+s\sum_a q_aT_a. \]

Taking \(\operatorname{Tr}\Pi^2=1\) yields

\[ 1=\frac1F+\frac12\sum_aq_a^2,\qquad \sum_aq_a^2=\frac{2(F-1)}F. \]

For \(F>2\), this length condition is necessary but not sufficient. The full orbit retains \(\Pi^2=\Pi\), or equivalently \((K_\gamma+\gamma I)^2=s(K_\gamma+\gamma I)\). This is the precise geometry shared with generalized spin mapping.

1.6 Deriving the Sphere Measure and Its Moments

Now the integral needs an actual measure. Equal treatment of all electronic bases calls for the invariant uniform measure on the sphere, with the common phase integrated out or left as a normalized redundant coordinate. In Cartesian coordinates it is

\[ d\mu_\gamma(x,p)= \frac{(F-1)!}{(2\pi)^F s^{F-1}}\, \delta\!\left(\sum_nJ_n-s\right) \prod_n dx_n\,dp_n. \]

The normalization can be checked without guessing a sphere volume. Put \(r^2=\sum_n(x_n^2+p_n^2)\), use the angular area \(2\pi^F/(F-1)!\), and integrate the radial delta function:

\[ \begin{aligned} \int\delta(r^2/2-s)\,d^{2F}y &=\frac{2\pi^F}{(F-1)!} \int_0^\infty r^{2F-1}\delta(r^2/2-s)\,dr\\ &=\frac{2\pi^F}{(F-1)!}(2s)^{F-1} =\frac{(2\pi)^F s^{F-1}}{(F-1)!}. \end{aligned} \]

Thus \(\int d\mu_\gamma=1\). In terms of the unit direction \(u=z/\sqrt{s}\), it becomes the same normalized invariant measure \(d\mu(u)\) for every radius. The two moments needed for operator reconstruction follow from this invariance.

First, \(\int\Pi\,d\mu\) commutes with every unitary, so it is \(cI\). Its trace is one, giving \(c=1/F\). For the second moment, define \(M_2=\int\Pi\otimes\Pi\,d\mu\). Unitary invariance places it in the span of \(I\) and the swap operator \(\mathsf S\), where \(\mathsf S(v\otimes w)=w\otimes v\). Each \(u\otimes u\) is symmetric, so \(M_2=c(I+\mathsf S)\). Since \(\operatorname{Tr}M_2=1\),

\[ \int\Pi\,d\mu=\frac IF,\qquad \int\Pi\otimes\Pi\,d\mu =\frac{I+\mathsf S}{F(F+1)}. \]

Contract the second identity with \(A\otimes B\). Because \(\operatorname{Tr}[(A\otimes B)\mathsf S]=\operatorname{Tr}(AB)\), we obtain the scalar relation that will fix the dual:

\[ \int (u^\dagger Au)(u^\dagger Bu)\,d\mu =\frac{\operatorname{Tr}A\,\operatorname{Tr}B+ \operatorname{Tr}(AB)} {F(F+1)}. \]

The integral is not an assumption that the electronic state is uniformly mixed. The measure describes the representation domain; the initial density contributes a separate symbol and weight.

1.7 Deriving the Dual and Reading Physical Information

The dual is fixed by the trace identity, not by a fit to dynamics and not by taking a matrix inverse of \(K_\gamma\). The same covariance argument permits \(\bar K=c\Pi+dI\). Write \(A_u=u^\dagger Au\), \(B_u=u^\dagger Bu\), and \(T_A=\operatorname{Tr}A\), \(T_B=\operatorname{Tr}B\). The original and dual symbols are \(a_\gamma=sA_u-\gamma T_A\) and \(\bar b=cB_u+dT_B\). Using the moments just derived gives

\[ \begin{aligned} F\int a_\gamma\bar b\,d\mu &=\frac{sc}{F+1}\operatorname{Tr}(AB)\\ &\quad+\left[ \frac{sc}{F+1}+sd-\gamma c-F\gamma d \right]T_AT_B. \end{aligned} \]

For this to equal \(\operatorname{Tr}(AB)\) for arbitrary \(A,B\), the first coefficient must be one and the second zero. Since \(s-F\gamma=1\), the two conditions reduce to

\[ \frac{sc}{F+1}=1,\qquad 1+d-\gamma c=0. \]

Solving them produces the matched dual:

\[ \boxed{\bar K_\gamma(u) =\frac{F+1}{s}\Pi(u) -\frac{1-\gamma}{s}I}. \]

It also has trace one. If we write it in the same affine family, its parameter is

\[ \bar\gamma=\frac{1-\gamma}{1+F\gamma}, \qquad 1+F\bar\gamma=\frac{F+1}{1+F\gamma}. \]

This relation means that the dual evaluates the same direction at an inverse scale. It does not instruct us to launch another trajectory at the dual parameter \(\bar\gamma\). The “inverse mapping kernel” terminology in the CPS literature refers to this reconstruction role. [3]

Because the trace pairing holds for every test operator, it gives both reconstruction formulas:

\[ \begin{aligned} A&=F\int \operatorname{Tr}[AK_\gamma(u)] \bar K_\gamma(u)\,d\mu,\\ A&=F\int \operatorname{Tr}[A\bar K_\gamma(u)] K_\gamma(u)\,d\mu. \end{aligned} \]

For an initial density \(\rho_0\) and observable \(O\), the physical expectation is consequently

\[ \langle O\rangle_0 =F\int f_{\rho_0,\gamma}(u)\, \bar o_\gamma(u)\,d\mu, \quad f_{\rho_0,\gamma}=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_0K_\gamma), \quad \bar o_\gamma=\operatorname{Tr}(O\bar K_\gamma). \]

This answers what the kernel does. It converts density and observable operators into scalar data; the invariant integral and matched dual recombine those data into a physical trace. No individual sample is required to be a physical population vector.

For the pure initial state \(\rho_0=C_k\), the explicit initial symbol and final population readout are

\[ f_{\rho_0,\gamma}(u)=s|u_k|^2-\gamma,\qquad \bar c_n(u)=\frac{F+1}{s}|u_n|^2 -\frac{1-\gamma}{s}. \]

Uniform Monte Carlo sampling therefore averages \(Ff_{\rho_0,\gamma}\bar c_n\), not ordinary populations with unit weights. The initial weight integrates to one because \(F\int f_{\rho_0,\gamma}d\mu=\operatorname{Tr}\rho_0=1\). Signed weights are possible. A pure electronic state does not collapse this full-sphere representation to one point, but Monte Carlo does not require visiting every point of a continuum; importance sampling and suitable quadrature are alternatives.

1.8 Why Self-Duality Singles Out W

If every allowed \(\gamma\) has a correct dual, what distinguishes one value? Exact trace reconstruction alone does not distinguish them. Self-duality is an additional symmetry requirement: the same kernel should represent both factors of the operator pairing.

Requiring \(K_\gamma=\bar K_\gamma\) matches their rank-one coefficients, giving \(s=(F+1)/s\). On the positive Cartesian branch,

\[ s_{\mathrm W}=\sqrt{F+1},\qquad \gamma_{\mathrm W}=\frac{\sqrt{F+1}-1}{F}. \]

It is useful to check this through the whole reconstruction map. If we deliberately use the original kernel on both sides, the same moments give

\[ F\int \operatorname{Tr}(AK_\gamma)K_\gamma\,d\mu =\frac{\operatorname{Tr}A}{F}I +\frac{s^2}{F+1} \left(A-\frac{\operatorname{Tr}A}{F}I\right). \]

The identity part is correct for all radii, but every traceless component is multiplied by \(s^2/(F+1)\). W is exactly where that multiplier becomes one. At another radius, using the same kernel without a compensating construction is a readout error even at time zero.

In the usual Stratonovich–Weyl notation, Q has \(\gamma_{\mathrm Q}=0\), P has \(\gamma_{\mathrm P}=1\), and these are dual partners. W is its own dual. Generalized spin mapping uses this \(SU(F)\) geometry and its distinguished W scale. The group-theory statement agrees with the radius calculation: define \(k_a=\operatorname{Tr}(T_aK_\gamma)=sq_a/2\), then

\[ \sum_a k_a^2=\frac{s^2(F-1)}{2F}. \]

The fundamental-representation quadratic Casimir satisfies \(\sum_aT_a^2=(F^2-1)I/(2F)\). Matching the classical component length to this value gives \(s^2=F+1\). Thus the Casimir and self-duality identify the same W construction in these conventions. [6]

Self-duality removes a representation asymmetry. It does not prove optimal approximate nuclear dynamics. Other radii still reconstruct operators exactly when paired with their own duals; their estimator variance and classical feedback can differ.

1.9 An Electronic Reconstruction Check

A compact calculation isolates the benefit of a correct dual before introducing nuclear feedback. Use the two-state Hamiltonian and coherent initial state

\[ V=\begin{pmatrix} 0.35&0.23+0.17i\\ 0.23-0.17i&0.90 \end{pmatrix},\qquad |\psi_0\rangle= \begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{0.7}\\\sqrt{0.3}\,e^{0.4i}\end{pmatrix}. \]

The reference is \(e^{-iVt}\rho_0e^{iVt}\). Independently integrate the initial symbol and propagated dual over \(\mathbb{CP}^1\), using 6 Gauss–Legendre polar nodes and 12 azimuths, then refine to 10 and 20. The check uses \(t=0,0.7,1.4,2.1\) and \(\gamma=-0.2,0,\gamma_{\mathrm W},1/2,1\), with tolerance \(10^{-12}\) for the full complex density, trace pairing, normalization, and population refinement.

Every matched dual passes: the maximum density error is \(7.8\times10^{-16}\), and the maximum population change under refinement is \(5.6\times10^{-16}\). Replacing the dual by the original kernel passes only at W. For example, using the ordinary MMST offset, the exact initial population 0.7 becomes

\[ 0.5+\frac43(0.7-0.5)=0.766\ldots \]

under that mismatched readout, already before propagation.

The dual therefore helps by removing a calculable representation error. The check is deterministic electronic reconstruction, not evidence of accurate coupled nuclear trajectories. Its numerical receipt, population table, and reproducible script retain the matched and deliberately mismatched calculations.

Chapter 2 — From Exact Symbols to Approximate Trajectories

2.1 What the Electronic Poisson Bracket Gets Exactly Right

Chapter 1 established an exact representation, but a simulation also needs a generator. The usual trajectory choice is the scalar Hamiltonian

\[ h_\gamma(R,P,z) =\frac{P^2}{2M}+z^\dagger V(R)z -\gamma\operatorname{Tr}V(R). \]

With canonical brackets \(\{x_n,p_m\}=\delta_{nm}\), the complex amplitudes satisfy \(\{z_n,z_m^*\}=-i\delta_{nm}\). For scalar functions, the electronic bracket is

\[ \{a,b\}_{e} =-i\sum_n \left(\frac{\partial a}{\partial z_n} \frac{\partial b}{\partial z_n^*} -\frac{\partial a}{\partial z_n^*} \frac{\partial b}{\partial z_n}\right). \]

Take electronic operators \(A,B\), independent of nuclear variables, with symbols \(a_\gamma=z^\dagger Az-\gamma\operatorname{Tr}A\) and \(b_\gamma=z^\dagger Bz-\gamma\operatorname{Tr}B\). Differentiation gives \(\partial_{z_n}a_\gamma=(z^\dagger A)_n\) and \(\partial_{z_n^*}a_\gamma=(Az)_n\). Substituting them yields

\[ \begin{aligned} \{a_\gamma,b_\gamma\}_{e} &=-i\left(z^\dagger ABz-z^\dagger BAz\right)\\ &=-iz^\dagger[A,B]z =\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[-i[A,B]K_\gamma\right]. \end{aligned} \]

The last equality uses \(\operatorname{Tr}[A,B]=0\). This is an exact correspondence of the electronic commutator algebra with the quadratic-symbol Poisson bracket. For the potential Hamiltonian it gives

\[ i\dot z=V(R)z,\qquad \dot K_\gamma=-i[V(R),K_\gamma]. \]

Hermiticity conserves \(z^\dagger z=s\). The trajectory therefore remains on its sphere without an extra projection. In a real symmetric diabatic basis, the same equation reads \(\dot x=Vp\), \(\dot p=-Vx\).

This exact commutator correspondence is why it is misleading to say simply that “using a Poisson bracket makes electronic evolution wrong.” The missing issue is operator multiplication coupled to nuclear derivatives.

2.2 Where the Coupled Generator Differs

The commutator calculation does not establish \((AB)_\gamma=a_\gamma b_\gamma\). Define the induced electronic product by \((a_\gamma\star_e b_\gamma)(u)=\operatorname{Tr}[ABK_\gamma(u)]\). Its antisymmetric part is represented correctly by the electronic bracket, but its symmetric part is generally not ordinary scalar multiplication.

A projector gives the simplest check. Since \(C_n^2=C_n\), its product symbol equals \(c_n\). Its ordinary scalar square equals \(c_n^2\), which differs whenever \(c_n\) is not zero or one. Thus a trajectory coordinate can preserve the electronic Lie algebra without preserving the whole quantum operator algebra.

To see why that matters for nuclear motion, take one nuclear coordinate and the matrix Hamiltonian \(H(R,P)=P^2I/(2M)+V(R)\). A nuclear Wigner transform followed by the first-order nuclear gradient expansion gives the adjoint quantum–classical Liouville equation for an observable matrix \(B(R,P,t)\):

\[ \partial_t B =i[V,B]+\frac PM\partial_R B -\frac12\left[ (\partial_RV)(\partial_PB) +(\partial_PB)(\partial_RV)\right]. \]

The nuclear force term contains the operator anticommutator. Full quantum nuclear propagation can have further Wigner–Moyal terms; even before those terms are considered, the displayed matrix equation differs from scalar Hamiltonian flow. Mapping the matrix equation gives

\[ (\partial_t B)_\gamma =\{b_\gamma,h_\gamma\}_{e} +\frac PM\partial_R b_\gamma -\frac12 \left[(\partial_RV)(\partial_PB) +(\partial_PB)(\partial_RV)\right]_\gamma. \]

The independent scalar trajectories instead generate

\[ \dot b_\gamma =\{b_\gamma,h_\gamma\}_{e} +\frac PM\partial_R b_\gamma -(\partial_Rv_\gamma)(\partial_Pb_\gamma), \quad v_\gamma=\operatorname{Tr}(VK_\gamma). \]

The first two terms agree. The last replaces a symmetrized operator product by a product of scalar symbols. The CPS review makes this distinction explicitly when comparing its mixed quantum–classical limit with the ordinary full Poisson bracket. [5, supplementary Appendix 5]

A concrete counterexample. Let \(F=2\), \(V(R)=R\sigma_z\), and \(B(R,P)=P\sigma_z\), where \(\sigma_z=\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)\). In the matrix equation, the commutator and position derivative vanish, while \(\partial_RV=\partial_PB=\sigma_z\). Therefore

\[ \partial_tB=-\frac12(\sigma_z^2+\sigma_z^2) =-I,\qquad (\partial_tB)_\gamma=-1. \]

Write \(\Pi=(I+\boldsymbol n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)/2\), with \(|\boldsymbol n|=1\), so \(K_\gamma=(I+s\boldsymbol n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)/2\). Put \(q=\operatorname{Tr}(\sigma_zK_\gamma)=sn_z\). The scalar symbols are \(h_\gamma=P^2/(2M)+Rq\) and \(b_\gamma=Pq\); their electronic bracket vanishes. The nuclear bracket gives

\[ \{Pq,h_\gamma\}_{R,P}=-q^2=-s^2n_z^2. \]

At an equatorial direction, this is zero, while the correct matrix generator gives minus one. They are different generators. Special ensemble moments can cancel this difference for a particular initial state or instant; the example does not claim every first averaged derivative is wrong. It does rule out a general pointwise equivalence. Because the potential is linear and the scalar kinetic term quadratic, this example does not require omitted higher nuclear Moyal derivatives to produce the discrepancy.

Consequently, a matched dual can reconstruct the operator information present in the propagated representation, but cannot turn this substituted generator into the exact one. Adding more samples controls integration error, not the missing operator-product terms.

2.3 Prescribed Nuclear Paths Versus Nuclear Feedback

First prescribe one nuclear path \(R(t)\). Every electronic sample then sees the same matrix \(V(R(t))\), with a common unitary \(U(t)\) solving \(i\dot U=V(R(t))U\), \(U(0)=I\). The trajectory and both kernels obey

\[ z(t)=U(t)z(0),\quad K_\gamma(t)=U(t)K_\gamma(0)U^\dagger(t),\quad \bar K_\gamma(t)=U(t)\bar K_\gamma(0)U^\dagger(t). \]

Insert the propagated dual into the initial reconstruction. The same \(U(t)\) can be taken outside the integral:

\[ \begin{aligned} F\int f_{\rho_0,\gamma}(u) \bar K_\gamma(u_t)\,d\mu &=U(t)\left[ F\int f_{\rho_0,\gamma}(u) \bar K_\gamma(u)\,d\mu \right]U^\dagger(t)\\ &=U(t)\rho_0U^\dagger(t). \end{aligned} \]

This proves exact electronic evolution along the prescribed path, for every allowed radius with its matched representation and readout. The path can be time dependent; “frozen nuclei” is only a special case. The statement concerns the quantum electronic subsystem driven by that path, not the quantum validity of the supplied nuclear path itself.

Now let each mapping sample drive its own classical nuclei. The canonical equations add

\[ \dot R_\alpha=\frac{P_\alpha}{M},\qquad \dot P_\alpha =-\operatorname{Tr} [K_\gamma(u_\alpha)\nabla_RV(R_\alpha)]. \]

The resulting \(R_\alpha(t)\), and hence \(U_\alpha(t)\), depend on the electronic sample. Reconstruction becomes an integral of different unitaries:

\[ \rho_{\mathrm{traj}}(t) =F\int f_{\rho_0,\gamma}(u)\, U_u(t)\bar K_\gamma(u)U_u^\dagger(t)\,d\mu. \]

There is no common unitary to factor out. The proof above no longer applies; the operator-product discrepancy in Section 2.2 explains the missing generator structure. In the full quantum problem, electron–nuclear correlations, branching, and nuclear coherence also belong to the evolving joint state. Independent classical paths do not generally retain them.

A focused pure-state representation makes the boundary intuitive. For \(\gamma\ge0\), choose \(J_k=1+\gamma\), \(J_{n\ne k}=\gamma\), and independent uniform phases. Then \(\langle K_\gamma(0)\rangle=C_k\). Along a common nuclear path, linearity gives \(\langle K_\gamma(t)\rangle=UC_kU^\dagger\), using the original kernel as this focused construction's readout. With sample-dependent nuclear feedback, the initial first-moment identity no longer proves equivalence to full-sphere sampling with its dual.

The practical boundary is therefore exact driven-electronic propagation versus generally approximate self-consistent electron–nuclear propagation. It is not a no-go theorem: decoupled systems and particular closed limits can be exact, and CPS can support more complete dynamics than independent Hamiltonian trajectories.

2.4 MMST and Spin Mapping Viewed Through CPS

At \(\gamma=1/2\), the CPS scalar Hamiltonian is the ordinary MMST quadratic Hamiltonian. Fix the same initial point, potential, and integrator: the electronic equations, nuclear force, conserved total action, and trajectory are identical. The name CPS does not alter that evolution.

What changes in a complete protocol can be the domain and measure, the initial symbol, the treatment of the identity, and the readout. CPS builds unit trace into every force kernel. An unprojected full-Cartesian MMST sampling can have different radii; focused MMST can already fix the radius. Exact MMST with its projection and correct operator symbols can also recover exact observables. There is no general improvement in exact reconstruction merely from renaming the representation.

A useful pointwise difference concerns a common electronic potential. Write \(V(R)=v_0(R)I+\widetilde V(R)\), with \(v_0=\operatorname{Tr}V/F\). In scalar CPS,

\[ (v_0I)_\gamma=v_0,\qquad \dot P_0=-\nabla_Rv_0. \]

In a direct unprojected Cartesian MMST classicalization, the same contribution can instead be \(v_0(\sum_nJ_n-F/2)\). A geometry-dependent common potential then gives a fluctuating force coefficient when the radial sampling fluctuates. The trace-one sphere prevents that artifact at each point. Separating the electronic identity or fixing the norm can repair MMST too; Part IV's focused protocol already has a fixed total action. A constant energy offset creates no force, so it would not test this difference.

Generalized spin mapping starts from coherent-state and Stratonovich–Weyl structure. In canonical Cartesian form, its usual W construction has the same rank-one orbit, the same scalar Hamiltonian form, and \(\gamma=\gamma_{\mathrm W}\). In this specific sense, W spin mapping is contained in the scalar CPS family. Its \(F\)-dependent radius follows from self-duality/Casimir matching rather than assigning \(1/2\) to every oscillator. [6]

Changing only \(\gamma\) reproduces that Hamiltonian parameter choice. Reproducing a complete spin-mapping calculation additionally requires its initial ensemble, identity convention, operator estimator, and numerical propagation. Full-sphere W and focused W are distinct coupled trajectory protocols even when they use the same radius.

Thus CPS clarifies and unifies choices that were previously attached to particular derivations. Spin mapping supplies a distinguished symmetric choice within that comparison. Neither changes the general coupling error derived above simply by fixing a radius.

2.5 What a Fixed Radius Changes About ZPE Leakage

The remaining MMST concern is often described as “an oscillator falls below its zero-point energy.” In these formulas, however, \(J_n\) is a dimensionless action, not the energy of an independent physical oscillator. Off-diagonal electronic couplings and the matrix \(V(R)\) determine the energy. The mapping oscillator is auxiliary.

Hermitian electronic evolution conserves the total action \(\sum_nJ_n=s\), while allowing redistribution between modes. The raw diagonal symbol \(J_n-\gamma\) can become negative without loss of total action. It is not by itself evidence that a trajectory has acquired a literal forbidden Fock occupation. Exact sphere integration can still give a valid population.

The force issue is more concrete. At one fixed geometry diagonalize \(V\), with energies \(E_k\). Since the transformed actions remain nonnegative and sum to \(s\), the scalar potential is

\[ v_\gamma=\sum_k(J_k-\gamma)E_k,\qquad J_k\ge0,\quad \sum_kJ_k=s. \]

A linear function on this simplex is minimized by putting all action into the lowest level and maximized by putting it into the highest. Substituting \(s=1+F\gamma\) gives

\[ \begin{aligned} v_\gamma^{\min} &=sE_{\min}-\gamma\sum_kE_k =E_{\min}-\gamma\sum_k(E_k-E_{\min}),\\ v_\gamma^{\max} &=sE_{\max}-\gamma\sum_kE_k =E_{\max}+\gamma\sum_k(E_{\max}-E_k). \end{aligned} \]

Positive \(\gamma\) permits a scalar potential below the lowest adiabatic energy and signed contributions to the nuclear force. Fixing the norm does not remove this. Smaller positive values reduce the possible extension, which is one reason a changed radius can affect approximate dynamics.

The bound is local in geometry. It does not establish that an initialized ensemble reaches an endpoint, or provide a universal nuclear-heating budget. Likewise, the negative eigenvalue of W does not prove that every spin-mapping result suffers a particular leakage artifact.

At \(\gamma=0\), the force kernel is a pure-state projector and its potential is inside the physical adiabatic interval. A focused calculation then becomes ordinary Ehrenfest. At negative allowed \(\gamma\), the raw force kernel is positive and its interval lies inside the adiabatic range, but the matched dual is signed. The exact readout can still return zero or one; it is not restricted to the diagonal range of the force kernel.

Removing a below-ground scalar potential does not restore the missing coupled generator or quantum detailed balance. Conversely, a signed single-trajectory symbol does not automatically invalidate an exact representation. ZPE-related dynamical error must be assessed through the actual force, ensemble observables, and a controlled reference, rather than negativity alone.

2.6 The Actual Trajectory Workflow

The mathematical pieces now specify an implementable calculation. For clarity, take a factorized initial state \(\rho_N\otimes\rho_e\), a chosen nuclear phase-space distribution \(f_N(R_0,P_0)\), and a fixed-basis electronic observable \(O\). The following is the full-sphere scalar CPS approximation, often described as extended classical mapping in the corresponding formulation:

  1. Choose the representation and readout. Select \(F\), \(\gamma>-1/F\), \(s=1+F\gamma\), and the matched \(\bar K_\gamma\). Choosing W makes them identical. This specifies symbols; it is not yet a propagation accuracy claim.
  2. Prepare the nuclear ensemble. Sample \((R_0,P_0)\) from the declared \(f_N\). A thermal Wigner distribution and a classical Boltzmann distribution are different preparations. If a signed nuclear distribution is used, retain its weight as well.
  3. Sample electronic directions. For uniform sampling, draw independent real and imaginary Gaussian components, normalize to \(u^\dagger u=1\), and set \(z_0=\sqrt{s}\,u_0\). This gives the invariant measure, rather than a radial Gaussian distribution.
  4. Attach the initial electronic weight. Compute \(w_e=F\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_eK_\gamma(u_0))\). For a proposal density \(q(u)\) relative to \(d\mu\), use \(w_e=F\operatorname{Tr}(\rho_eK_\gamma)/q(u)\), with support wherever needed. A basis pure state uses the explicit formula in Section 1.7.
  5. Propagate each sample. Integrate \(i\dot z=V(R)z\), \(\dot R=P/M\), and \(\dot P=-\operatorname{Tr}(K_\gamma\nabla_RV)\). A unitary electronic step preserves the sphere; a coupled integrator still needs time-step convergence. The force uses the Hamiltonian kernel \(K_\gamma\), not an arbitrarily substituted dual.
  6. Read the observable with the declared estimator. For full-sphere sampling, evaluate \(\bar o_\alpha(t)=\operatorname{Tr}[O\bar K_\gamma(u_\alpha(t))]\) and average \(w_{e,\alpha}\bar o_\alpha(t)\), together with any nuclear weights.
  7. Separate convergence from physical accuracy. Check weight normalization, norm and energy diagnostics, time-step refinement, and independent sampling batches. Compare the final observable with a quantum or controlled reference to assess the dynamical approximation.

The population formula for uniform electronic sampling and equally sampled positive nuclear initial conditions is explicitly

\[ \langle C_n\rangle_{\mathrm{traj}}(t) \approx\frac1N\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N} F\left(s|u_{\alpha,k}(0)|^2-\gamma\right) \left[ \frac{F+1}{s}|u_{\alpha,n}(t)|^2 -\frac{1-\gamma}{s} \right] \]

for the initial state \(C_k\). “Equal averaging” here means equal averaging of weighted contributions. It does not mean that each trajectory has weight one and supplies a physical population. For geometry-dependent observables, use the consistently transformed operator \(O(R_\alpha(t))\); an ensemble population requires a shared basis or a clearly specified local projector.

In a focused protocol, replace the full-sphere initialization and dual formula by its own moment-matching initialization and estimator. Do not combine a focused ensemble with the full-sphere dual merely because both carry the same \(\gamma\). In ordinary focused initialization, negative \(\gamma\) would require negative inactive actions and is unavailable; that does not invalidate the full-sphere representation at negative \(\gamma\).

For a parameter comparison, pair the nuclear initial conditions and hold the observable, timestep accuracy, and uncertainty target fixed. A change of radius changes forces as well as readout variance. Selecting \(\gamma\) against one observable can improve that approximate calculation, but its transferability to other observables, temperatures, and long-time behavior needs an independent test.

2.7 CPS, MMST, Spin Mapping, and Ehrenfest Side by Side

The useful comparison is between complete protocols. All four can propagate electronic amplitudes exactly along a prescribed nuclear path. Their main differences appear in initialization, force kernels, estimators, and the coupled closure.

For a pure initial electronic state, focused \(\gamma=0\) gives \(K=\rho=zz^\dagger\), \(z^\dagger z=1\), and exactly the Ehrenfest equations and readout. A generic full-sphere Q calculation instead uses a Q initial symbol and P dual, with different sample-dependent forces; it is not automatically ordinary focused Ehrenfest. A mixed-state Ehrenfest calculation can propagate its density directly, and need not equal an ensemble of pure-state decompositions after nonlinear nuclear feedback.

At a matched radius, Hamiltonian, initial ensemble, and estimator, CPS and a corresponding MMST implementation can coincide. W spin mapping adds a particular geometry and symmetric pairing; Spin-MInt adds a propagation scheme. Improvements seen between unmatched protocols cannot be assigned to “the mapping theory” alone.

The answer to what CPS improves is therefore concrete. It provides a finite-state constrained representation, pointwise normalization of the identity, an explicit dual for operator recovery, and a systematic account of parameter choices. It also identifies the boundary between exact electronic algebra and approximate coupled trajectories. Accurate nuclear feedback and quantum equilibrium remain properties to demonstrate for a specified dynamical method.
